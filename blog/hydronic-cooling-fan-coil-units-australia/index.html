Hydronic Cooling with Fan Coil Units in Australia: Why Your Radiators Can't Do It (But These Can)

Most people know hydronic heating. Far fewer know that the same system can cool your home in summer, if it has the right terminal units.

Hydronic cooling with fan coil units is one of the least-talked-about upgrades available to Australian hydronic homeowners, and one of the most useful. If you already have a hydronic heating system, you're closer to year-round climate control than you probably realise. The pipes are there. The heat pump can run in reverse. The missing piece, for most homes, is the terminal unit, and that's where fan coil units come in.

Before we get to the solution, it's worth understanding why your existing radiators can't do the job, and why that's a physics problem rather than a product limitation.

Why hydronic radiators can't cool your home

Radiators work brilliantly for heating. Warm water circulates through the panel, radiates heat into the room, and returns to the boiler or heat pump to be reheated. Simple, silent, effective.

Run that system in reverse, circulating cold water through the same radiator panel, and you immediately hit a problem: condensation.

Cold metal surfaces attract moisture from the air. When the surface temperature of a radiator drops below the dew point of the surrounding air, the temperature at which water vapour starts to condense, water begins forming on the panel. In a Sydney summer, with humidity above 60% and indoor air around 26°C, the dew point can be 17 to 18°C or higher. A cooling system trying to deliver meaningful comfort would need to circulate water significantly colder than that.

The result: water streaming down your radiator panels, pooling on the floor, and potentially causing mould and water damage. Hydronic cooling through radiators is physically possible in very dry climates with low dew points, but in most of Australia, particularly coastal NSW, Victoria in summer, and Queensland, it isn't a workable solution.

This is why hydronic cooling needs a different type of terminal unit entirely.

How fan coil units actually work

Fan coil units solve the condensation problem by managing it deliberately rather than ignoring it.

A fan coil unit is a compact unit, typically wall-mounted, a ceiling cassette, or ducted, containing a coil of pipe through which chilled water circulates, and a fan that draws room air across that coil. The chilled coil cools the air, which is then blown back into the room.

Because the coil is enclosed within the unit, any condensation that forms on it is captured in a drip tray and drained away through a condensate pipe, exactly the way a conventional split-system air conditioner manages condensation. The room stays dry. The floor stays dry. The system can operate with water temperatures well below the dew point without any risk of surface moisture.

The result is genuine, effective cooling, delivered through your hydronic system and powered by the same heat pump that heats your home in winter.

Fan coil options for different homes

The right fan coil unit for your home depends on your ceiling height, room layout, and what you want it to look like. The three most common configurations:

  • Wall-mounted units, similar in appearance to a standard split system indoor unit, mounted high on the wall. Good for individual rooms, easy to retrofit, straightforward to zone independently.
  • Ceiling cassettes, recessed into the ceiling void, with grilles that sit flush with the ceiling. More discreet than wall units, with good airflow distribution for larger open-plan spaces.
  • Ducted fan coil, a single unit connected to a duct network, delivering conditioned air to several rooms through ceiling vents. Closest to a traditional ducted air conditioning look, and best suited to homes with accessible ceiling space.

Zoning and control

Fan coil units can be zoned independently. Each unit has its own thermostat and can be controlled separately, so you're not cooling the whole house when you only need the bedroom at night, or the living area in the afternoon. The control system manages the whole heating and cooling schedule, planning charging around your solar forecast and your tariff.

The efficiency advantage

Conventional split-system air conditioning runs refrigerant, under high pressure, in copper lines, all the way from the outdoor unit to every indoor head. The refrigerant cycle is efficient, but it runs at much colder temperatures than are necessary for human comfort, which drives up energy use.

Hydronic cooling circulates chilled water, a simpler and lower-pressure fluid, from a central heat pump to each fan coil unit. The heat pump runs at a higher evaporating temperature than a conventional split system, which improves its efficiency. And because the thermal store absorbs the cooling load, the heat pump can charge it when power is cheapest rather than in the peak of the afternoon heat.

In practice, a well-designed hydronic cooling system with thermal storage can deliver summer comfort on the cheapest energy of the day, the same way it delivers heating and hot water in winter.

What to expect from the performance

Fan coil units are not identical to a conventional split system in how the cooling feels. A few things worth knowing:

  • Response time is slightly slower. Chilled water systems take a little longer to reach target temperature than direct refrigerant systems, because the thermal mass of the water loop has to cool down first. This is why the control system pre-cools the thermal store in advance, so stored cooling is ready when you need it rather than starting from warm when you get home.
  • Airflow is gentler. Fan coil units typically move air more gently than high-capacity split systems. That is usually experienced as more comfortable, not less: no cold blasts, just gradual, even cooling. The tradeoff is that they suit holding a comfortable temperature better than rapidly cooling a very hot room.
  • Humidity management. Like split systems, fan coil units dehumidify as they cool, because moisture condenses on the coil and drains away. In a humid Australian summer, that dehumidification is a significant part of what makes a space feel comfortable.

The short version

Your hydronic radiators can heat your home beautifully. They cannot cool it: condensation on the panels below the dew point makes that unworkable in most Australian conditions.

Fan coil units solve this. They are designed to manage condensation internally, which means chilled water can circulate through them without any surface moisture risk.
