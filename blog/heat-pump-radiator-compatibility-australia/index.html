Heat Pump Radiator Compatibility: The Flow-Temperature Question, Answered

The number one reason Australian hydronic homeowners don't call a heat pump installer. Here's what's actually going on, and a simple test you can do right now.

If you've looked into replacing your gas hydronic boiler with a heat pump, you've probably hit a wall somewhere around this claim: "heat pumps run at lower temperatures than gas boilers, so you might need to replace your radiators."

That sentence has stopped a lot of conversations cold. It sounds expensive. It sounds like someone is about to tell you your perfectly good heating system isn't good enough any more.

Here's the thing: it's often not true. And the answer is almost always more reassuring than people expect.

This article explains what flow temperature actually means, why it matters for heat pumps, and most usefully, how you can test your own system right now to find out whether your radiators are already capable of running well with a heat pump.

What is flow temperature, and why does it matter?

Your hydronic system works by circulating hot water from a heat source, your gas boiler or a heat pump, through your radiators or underfloor circuits and back again.

Flow temperature is the temperature of the water going out to your radiators. Return temperature is the water coming back once it has given up heat to the room.

Your gas boiler has probably been running at a flow temperature somewhere between 70 and 80°C. That's hot. Boilers can produce that efficiently because burning gas generates a lot of heat quickly.

Heat pumps work differently. They extract heat from the outside air and amplify it, like a refrigerator running in reverse. They are extraordinarily efficient at doing this, but they work best at lower temperatures. The sweet spot for most heat pumps is a flow temperature between 45 and 55°C. They can go higher, but efficiency drops the hotter they run.

This is where the anxiety comes from. Your boiler runs at 75°C. The heat pump runs at 50°C. Do your radiators still keep the house warm?

For many Australian homes, yes, and here's why.

Your radiators were probably oversized

When a hydronic system is designed, the engineer calculates how much heat your home needs on the coldest day of the year. Then, because engineers are cautious, they typically add a safety margin. And the installer often adds a bit more, because nobody wants a callback in July saying the house is cold.

The result is that most Australian radiator systems are generously sized. They were designed to run at 70 to 80°C, but they don't need to. They have capacity in reserve.

Think of it this way. If your radiator is rated to produce 1,000W at a flow temperature of 75°C, it might produce 750W at 55°C. But if your home only needs 600W to stay comfortable on a cold day, because your insulation, windows or orientation mean your heat loss is lower than the worst-case design scenario, then 750W is still more than enough. You would never notice the difference.

This is why the blanket "you might need to replace your radiators" claim is often wrong. Whether you need to depends entirely on your home, your radiators, and how cold it actually gets where you live.

The simple test you can run yourself

Here's something useful to know: if your gas boiler can keep your home comfortable while running at a flow temperature of 55°C or below on a cold day, your radiator system will work well with a heat pump. You don't need to replace anything.

The UK training organisation Heat Geek has been teaching this for years, and it applies here just as well. The logic is straightforward: if your radiators can do the job at 55°C, it doesn't matter whether the heat source is a gas flame or a heat pump. The radiators don't know the difference.

Here's how to test it:

  1. Wait for a genuinely cold day, ideally below 10°C outside, which for Melbourne or alpine-fringe homes means winter. For Sydney or Brisbane homes your heating load is lower, so the test applies even at milder temperatures.
  2. Find the flow temperature dial on your gas boiler. On most modern condensing boilers it's on the front panel, often labelled with a radiator symbol or "CH" for central heating. It may be a rotary dial or a digital setting.
  3. Turn the flow temperature down slowly. Don't make a big drop at once. Knock it down by 5°C, then let the system run for 30 to 60 minutes and see how the house feels.
  4. Keep going, gradually, until either the house starts to feel less comfortable, or you reach 55°C. If you reach 55°C and the house is still warm, you have just confirmed your radiator system is heat pump ready.

A few notes on running this test:

  • Do it on a cold day. Testing on a mild day tells you nothing useful. Your system could run on a candle and keep the house warm at 18°C outside.
  • Be patient. Hydronic systems change temperature slowly. Give each setting at least an hour before judging it.
  • Watch your hot water. If your boiler also heats your domestic hot water, as most standard non-combi boilers do, there is a minimum flow temperature you can go to while still heating the tank safely, typically 55 to 60°C. Below that you risk legionella growth in the cylinder. A heat pump installation typically separates hot water from space heating, which is why this stops being a limitation once you make the switch.

What if the house gets cold before you hit 55°C?

This doesn't mean you can't have a heat pump. It means there's a genuine conversation to be had about what the best solution looks like for your home. The usual options:

Zone optimisation. Sometimes only one or two rooms struggle, typically the largest room or one with a single small radiator. Upgrading those specific radiators, or adding a panel, is a straightforward and inexpensive fix that brings the whole system into range.

Running a little hotter. Most heat pumps can run at 60 to 65°C. Efficiency drops as the flow temperature rises, but it is still considerably cheaper to run than a gas boiler, and still compatible with your existing radiators.

Underfloor circuits. If your home has underfloor heating alongside or instead of radiators, that is typically more compatible with heat pumps, not less. Underfloor circuits are designed to operate at 35 to 45°C, right in the sweet spot for heat pump efficiency.

The key point: there are almost always workable solutions. The "you'll need to replace everything" framing assumes a worst case that applies to a minority of Australian homes.

What thermal storage changes

There's a wrinkle in the standard heat pump discussion that doesn't get much attention. Even when a heat pump runs perfectly well at low flow temperatures, most systems still buy power when you need heat most: the morning warm-up and the evening comfort hours, which is also when Australian electricity prices peak.

Thermal storage changes that. Rather than running the heat pump reactively when you're cold, a system with thermal storage charges an insulated tank when power is cheapest, whether that's a free window, your own solar, or late-night off-peak. By the time evening comes there's enough stored heat to run your radiators through the night with the heat pump off through the expensive hours.

This doesn't change the flow temperature fundamentals, which still apply. It changes the economics. A standard heat pump replacement cuts your gas bill. A heat pump with thermal storage cuts your energy bill.

What a site assessment actually checks

All of the above is useful context, but every home is different. The definitive answer comes from a proper heat load calculation and a look at your existing system. A good assessment for hydronic replacement should include:

  • Flow temperature test, or a review of your boiler settings if you've already run the test above
  • Heat loss calculation, how much heating power your home actually needs on a design day, by zone
  • Radiator sizing check, comparing your existing radiators against the heat load at lower flow temperatures
  • Pipework inspection, the condition of existing circuits, pump, manifolds and expansion vessel
  • Thermal store placement, where the tank goes: utility room, garage, or an outside enclosure
  • Electrical supply check, whether your switchboard handles the heat pump load

The output should be a clear answer on radiator compatibility, and if any upgrades are needed they should be in a fixed quote upfront, not discovered on installation day.

The short version

  • Gas boilers run at 70 to 80°C. Heat pumps run best at 45 to 55°C.
  • Most Australian radiator systems are oversized and work fine at lower temperatures.
  • Test it yourself: gradually reduce your boiler's flow temperature on a cold day. If the house stays warm at 55°C, your system is heat pump ready.
  • If it doesn't, the answer is usually zone optimisation or a couple of selective radiator upgrades, not a full replacement.
  • A site assessment gives you a definitive answer for your home.
